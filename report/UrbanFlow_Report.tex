\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{chap:intro}

\section{PURPOSE OF THE SYSTEM}
Urban transportation networks form the foundational circulatory infrastructure of modern cities. Efficient mobility facilitates economic productivity, access to employment, education, and healthcare. However, rapid global urbanization, coupled with expanding private vehicular ownership, has outpaced traditional transport infrastructure expansion. In rapidly developing metropolitan centers such as Kochi, Kerala, traffic congestion causes substantial losses in human productivity, elevated fuel waste, and severe vehicular exhaust emissions.

Traditional municipal traffic management strategies rely primarily on localized reactive interventions: widening narrow road segments, adjusting isolated traffic signal timers, or constructing capital-intensive flyovers. While intuitive, localized improvements frequently fail or paradoxically worsen overall network congestion. In 1968, mathematician Dietrich Braess demonstrated that adding an edge to a congested network can increase overall travel latency when independent agents choose selfish routes~\cite{braess1968}. To avert such adverse outcomes, contemporary urban planning requires rigorous computational graph modeling tools capable of simulating equilibrium dynamics, detecting latent bottlenecks, and discovering paradoxical network effects prior to costly physical construction.

\section{RELEVANCE AND SCOPE OF THE SYSTEM}

\subsection*{Relevance}
Road networks possess an intrinsic mathematical structure readily modeled as directed, weighted graphs. Graph theory provides an expressive and computationally tractable framework to model physical connectivity, non-linear flow-dependent latencies, and user-equilibrium flow distributions. In a transportation graph, intersection nodes act as vertices, road segments function as directed edges with finite physical capacities, and link traversal latencies scale non-linearly with traffic volume. Modern computational graph theory enables municipal planners to evaluate holistic, network-wide responses to local changes. By combining geographic information systems (GIS), shortest-path algorithms, multi-commodity flow formulations, and non-linear link performance curves, simulation engines can quantify how congestion propagates through interconnected corridors. This graph-centric methodology forms the core foundation of \textbf{UrbanFlow}.

\subsection*{Scope}
The primary scope of UrbanFlow encompasses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Extracting arterial road network topologies from OpenStreetMap (OSM) for Kochi, Kerala, including junction nodes, segment lengths, lanes, and functional road classes.
  \item Constructing directed multigraphs with non-linear latency models governed by the Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) link performance function.
  \item Simulating user-equilibrium traffic flow distributions using Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm combined with the Method of Successive Averages (MSA).
  \item Subjecting the network to comparative stress testing under both 100\% baseline demand and 200\% heavy congestion shock regimes.
  \item Identifying system bottlenecks, critical cut edges, and latent Braess Paradox shortcuts.
  \item Providing a combinatorial intervention optimizer that ranks actionable infrastructure modifications (such as one-way conversions, capacity expansion, and link closures).
  \item Delivering an interactive web-based GIS dashboard for dynamic visualization and scenario simulation.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{PROBLEM DEFINITION}
\label{chap:problem}

\section{PROBLEM STATEMENT}
Given a directed urban road network represented as a graph $G = (V, E)$, with edge capacities $C_e > 0$, free-flow traversal latencies $t_e^0 > 0$, and a multi-commodity origin-destination demand matrix $D = \{(s_i, d_i, q_i)\}_{i=1}^M$, the objective is to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Determine the Wardropian user-equilibrium flow distribution $x^* = \{x_e\}_{e \in E}$ such that no driver can unilaterally reduce their travel time by choosing an alternative route.
  \item Quantify systemic congestion under varying demand regimes (100\% baseline vs 200\% peak congestion shock) via non-linear link performance latency curves.
  \item Identify critical bottleneck edges where volume-to-capacity ($V/C$) ratios exceed acceptable thresholds ($V/C \ge 0.95$).
  \item Detect and resolve Braess Paradox occurrences where targeted edge modifications (e.g., one-way conversion or street closure) reduce overall network latency $\sum_{e \in E} x_e \cdot t_e(x_e)$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{OBJECTIVES}
To address this problem, UrbanFlow targets the following concrete engineering objectives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Objective 1 -- Graph Ingestion and Preprocessing:} Ingest real-world GIS road geometries from OpenStreetMap for the Kochi metropolitan arterial network, filter driveable road classes, consolidate multi-way intersections, and compute edge capacities based on lane counts and functional road classifications.
  \item \textbf{Objective 2 -- Equilibrium Traffic Assignment:} Implement the Method of Successive Averages (MSA) combined with Rust-accelerated Dijkstra shortest path routing to compute convergent user-equilibrium flow assignments.
  \item \textbf{Objective 3 -- Bottleneck and Cut Analysis:} Quantify link congestion using volume-to-capacity ($V/C$) ratios and link latency penalties to identify critical choke points and cut edges.
  \item \textbf{Objective 4 -- Braess Paradox and Combinatorial Optimization:} Develop a heuristic search module that evaluates candidate topological interventions (lane additions, one-way street conversions, and link removals) to maximize network-wide travel time savings.
  \item \textbf{Objective 5 -- Full-Stack GIS Visualization:} Implement a responsive, high-performance web dashboard featuring interactive map rendering, flow heatmaps, and before/after intervention comparison views.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{LITERATURE SURVEY}
\label{chap:literature}
Traffic assignment and network optimization have been foundational topics in operations research and algorithmic graph theory. In 1952, J.G. Wardrop formulated the two fundamental behavioral principles governing traffic distribution~\cite{wardrop1952}. Wardrop's First Principle (User Equilibrium) states that journey times on all actively utilized routes between an origin-destination pair are equal and minimal, such that no driver can unilaterally reduce travel time by switching paths. Wardrop's Second Principle (System Optimum) seeks to minimize the aggregate total travel time across all commuters. 

In decentralized transportation systems, commuters act selfishly, leading to a User Equilibrium rather than a System Optimum. The discrepancy between selfish routing and social optimum is quantified by the Price of Anarchy. In 1968, Dietrich Braess proved the famous paradox wherein the addition of a new road segment with zero marginal latency to a congested network can increase the equilibrium travel time for all commuters~\cite{braess1968}. In empirical computational networks, Sheffi established the Method of Successive Averages (MSA) as a mathematically guaranteed algorithm for solving non-linear convex equilibrium assignments~\cite{sheffi1985}. Modern advances in geospatial computation, notably Boeing's OSMnx framework~\cite{boeing2017osmnx}, have enabled the direct extraction of planar graph representations from OpenStreetMap data, providing the foundation for practical municipal graph engines.

\section{GRAPH DATASET DESCRIPTION AND PREPROCESSING}
Raw geographic data was acquired using the \texttt{OSMnx} Python package~\cite{boeing2017osmnx}, querying the Overpass API for drivable roadways in Kochi, Kerala. The raw network was filtered to extract primary, secondary, tertiary, and trunk roadways.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Node Consolidation:} Multi-way intersections with nearby centroid nodes ($< 15$ m) were consolidated into single representative junction vertices to prevent zero-distance algorithmic cycles.
  \item \textbf{Directionality and Edge Expansion:} Bidirectional roadways were expanded into dual anti-parallel directed edges $(u, v)$ and $(v, u)$, each with independent flow and capacity attributes.
  \item \textbf{Capacity and Speed Imputation:} Missing speed limits and lane counts were imputed following Indian Roads Congress (IRC:106) guidelines~\cite{irc106}, assigning nominal capacities of 900 vph/lane for secondary arterials and 1,200--1,800 vph for major multi-lane trunks.
  \item \textbf{Strong Connectivity Verification:} Tarjan's strongly connected components algorithm was executed to ensure all destination nodes remain reachable from any origin.
\end{enumerate}

\section{NON-LINEAR CONGESTION MODELLING: THE BPR FUNCTION}
To capture queue formation and delay decay as traffic volume $x_e$ approaches capacity $C_e$, UrbanFlow employs the Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) link performance function~\cite{bpr1964}:
\begin{equation}
  t_e(x_e) = t_e^0 \left[ 1 + \alpha_e \left( \frac{x_e}{C_e} \right)^{\beta_e} \right]
  \label{eq:bpr}
\end{equation}
where $t_e^0 = \frac{L_e}{v_e^0}$ is the free-flow traversal latency (hours), $\frac{x_e}{C_e}$ is the volume-to-capacity ($V/C$) ratio, and $\alpha_e = 0.15$ and $\beta_e = 4.0$ are the standard calibration exponents.

\caption{Non-Linear Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) Congestion Delay Curve}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bpr_curve}, for $V/C < 0.75$, travel time remains close to free-flow conditions. However, once $V/C \ge 1.0$, the fourth-power polynomial exponent induces an explosive growth in travel time, reflecting the severe physical delays of congested urban queueing.

\section{SHORTEST-PATH ROUTING AND EQUILIBRIUM ASSIGNMENT (MSA)}
Given updated link costs $w(e) = t_e(x_e)$, Dijkstra's single-source shortest path algorithm using a min-priority heap operates with time complexity $O(|E| + |V| \log |V|)$~\cite{dijkstra1959}. In user-equilibrium, no driver can unilaterally reduce their travel time. To guarantee convergence without wild flow oscillations, the \textbf{Method of Successive Averages (MSA)}~\cite{sheffi1985} updates flows using step size $\gamma_k = \frac{1}{k}$:
\begin{equation}
  x_e^{(k+1)} = x_e^{(k)} + \frac{1}{k+1} \left( y_e^{(k+1)} - x_e^{(k)} \right) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{k+1}\right) x_e^{(k)} + \frac{1}{k+1} y_e^{(k+1)}
\end{equation}
where $y_e^{(k+1)}$ is the all-or-nothing auxiliary flow. The algorithm terminates when relative gap $\epsilon^{(k)} \le 10^{-3}$ or maximum iterations ($K=30$) is reached:
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{(k)} = \frac{\sum_{e \in E} \left| x_e^{(k+1)} - x_e^{(k)} \right|}{\sum_{e \in E} x_e^{(k)}}
\end{equation}

\section{BRAESS PARADOX DISCOVERY AND COMBINATORIAL OPTIMIZATION}
The Braess Paradox occurs when a connecting shortcut induces drivers to divert from higher-capacity arterials, overwhelming bottleneck junctions and increasing average latency for all commuters~\cite{braess1968}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/braess_paradox_topology.pdf}
\caption{Classic 4-Node Diamond Topology Demonstrating Braess's Paradox}
\label{fig:braess_topology}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:braess_topology}, introducing the shortcut edge $X \to Y$ shifts users away from socially optimal paths to a congested Nash equilibrium. UrbanFlow implements a combinatorial search algorithm that systematically tests candidate modifications: edge closures ($G' = (V, E \setminus \{e\})$), one-way conversions ($C_e' = 1.4 \times C_e$), and capacity additions ($C_e' = C_e + \Delta C$). Each candidate network is re-assigned via MSA equilibrium, and interventions yielding the highest travel time savings are ranked.

\chapter{IMPLEMENTATION}
\label{chap:implementation}
UrbanFlow is engineered as a cloud-native microservices architecture comprising a high-performance Python simulation backend and an interactive React/MapLibre web GIS dashboard.

\section{BACKEND API AND SIMULATION ENGINE}
The simulation engine is implemented in Python~3.11 with FastAPI~\cite{fastapi}. The network topology is stored in an in-memory graph representation with vectorized NumPy arrays and Rust-accelerated shortest-path bindings (\texttt{rustworkx}). The backend exposes endpoints for equilibrium simulations (\texttt{/api/simulation/run}), bottleneck retrieval (\texttt{/api/analysis/bottlenecks}), and intervention evaluation (\texttt{/api/optimize/evaluate}). Listing~\ref{lst:urbanflow_core} illustrates the core Python implementation for BPR link latency calculation and MSA flow averaging.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={UrbanFlow Vectorized BPR Latency and MSA Assignment Routine}, label={lst:urbanflow_core}]
import numpy as np

def compute_bpr_latencies(lengths_km, speeds_kmh, flows_vph, capacities_vph, alpha=0.15, beta=4.0):
    """Vectorized calculation of non-linear BPR link traversal latencies."""
    t0_hours = lengths_km / np.maximum(speeds_kmh, 1e-3)
    vc_ratio = flows_vph / np.maximum(capacities_vph, 1e-3)
    return t0_hours * (1.0 + alpha * np.power(vc_ratio, beta))

def msa_equilibrium_assignment(edges, od_demands, max_iters=30, tolerance=1e-3):
    """Method of Successive Averages (MSA) User-Equilibrium Assignment."""
    flows = np.zeros(len(edges), dtype=np.float64)
    for k in range(1, max_iters + 1):
        costs = compute_bpr_latencies(edges['len'], edges['speed'], flows, edges['cap'])
        aux_flows = dijkstra_all_or_nothing(edges, costs, od_demands)
        new_flows = (1.0 - 1.0/k) * flows + (1.0/k) * aux_flows
        if np.sum(np.abs(new_flows - flows)) / np.maximum(np.sum(flows), 1.0) < tolerance:
            flows = new_flows; break
        flows = new_flows
    return flows, costs
\end{lstlisting}

\section{DISCUSSION}

\subsection*{Non-Linear Traffic Breakdown and Braess's Paradox}
The experimental results vividly illustrate the severe non-linearity of urban road congestion. While a naive linear model would predict a $2.0\times$ increase in delay when demand doubles, the actual physics of congested queues governed by the BPR function yields a $7.06\times$ surge in delay vehicle-hours. This finding reinforces the critical principle that transportation systems operate near critical thresholds: once capacity is breached, marginal vehicle injections induce catastrophic delays.

The optimizer's discovery of high-impact one-way conversions (such as Road 1048 yielding a 20.35\% delay reduction) provides vital guidance for municipal authorities. Many legacy urban shortcuts provide drivers with localized travel time reductions while inducing high conflict points and queue spillbacks at arterial merge junctions. Converting such links to unidirectional flow or closing them eliminates destructive selfish diversions, protecting the capacity of primary trunk corridors.

\subsection*{Computational Scalability and Operational Decision Support}
The simulation engine completes 30 MSA equilibrium iterations on the 490-node, 1,200-edge Kochi arterial network in 0.221--0.241 seconds on commodity hardware. This sub-second performance enables real-time interactive exploration in the web dashboard, allowing civil engineers to evaluate scenarios dynamically during planning workshops.

\chapter{CONCLUSION}
\label{chap:conclusion}
This project successfully developed and validated \textbf{UrbanFlow}, an advanced graph-theoretic traffic simulation and optimization engine. Formulated in accordance with the \textbf{\coursecode\ - \coursename} syllabus, the system models transportation networks as directed weighted graphs $G=(V, E)$ and simulates user-equilibrium traffic distributions using the Method of Successive Averages (MSA), Dijkstra routing, and the non-linear Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) function.

Applied to the real-world arterial road network of Kochi, Kerala (comprising 490 junctions and 1,200 road segments), the engine was evaluated under both 100\% baseline demand (27,300 vph) and 200\% heavy congestion shock (54,600 vph). The experimental results revealed the acute non-linear penalties of network congestion: doubling vehicular demand induced a 252.9\% surge in average travel time (from 21.84 minutes to 77.07 minutes) and an explosive $7.06\times$ rise in total network delay (from 9,935.2 to 70,137.7 vehicle-hours), expanding severely congested links from 112 to 295 segments.

Critically, UrbanFlow's optimization search engine demonstrated the practical significance of the Braess Paradox in metropolitan network management. By converting adverse shortcut roads into one-way corridors (notably on Road 1048), selfish shortcut diversion was eliminated, delivering a remarkable \textbf{20.35\% reduction} in citywide travel time under 200\% demand. Coordinated network modifications achieved a projected \textbf{30.69\% overall latency reduction}, reducing peak vehicle delay by over 21,500 vehicle-hours per hour. Supported by an interactive web GIS dashboard and sub-second computational runtimes, UrbanFlow provides transportation authorities with a robust, data-driven framework for sustainable urban mobility planning.
